Essays written from the lived perspective of a sensitive stomach that has very strong opinions, about how we eat, how we live in our bodies, and the traditions we inherit.

How I got to writing this

I came to food and culture writing sideways. I spent a couple of years in tech product management, which is a profession that teaches you to study how people behave — what they reach for by default, what they avoid, what they justify to themselves as rational when it's actually something older and less examined.

I left that work as it outpaced me, and took the habit of observation with me. The subject that kept appearing was food: not as nutrition, not as aesthetics, but as a record of how people actually live. What families cook on a weeknight. What gets packed in a tiffin box and why. What recipes survive the move to a new city and which ones don't make it.

My own body has been an unusually loud teacher. I cook and eat with minimal chilli, gluten, lactose, and foods that generate heat — not as a diet, but because my body made that decision for me. Working around those restrictions led me to Ayurveda — as a system that had already thought carefully about what the body does with food, when, and why. And so, my grounding is in Ayurveda.

What I write about is the space between those things: inherited knowledge and lived experiment, cultural practice and personal digestion, the food that comes from elsewhere and the body that receives it. I'm interested in what looks like less and turns out to be more.

One teaspoon short of inheritance

My mother never needed to measure, and I couldn't stop measuring. But for sukhadi, a Gujarati sweet of just three ingredients, she gave me the exact amounts. There was nowhere to hide. The first time, I couldn't believe ghee and flour went in almost a one-to-one ratio. Years of reading up on portion control made me think better answers existed somewhere far from home. On one side stood my fear of weight gain. On the other, my mother's craft.
